Furnace Repair

What does the furnace do when heat is requested, and does it stop at the same point each time?

A furnace that will not start, shuts down during a cycle, makes an unfamiliar sound, or leaves some rooms cooler needs diagnosis before parts are selected. The cause may involve ignition, a flame sensor, a pressure switch, a blower motor, airflow, or the thermostat, and these faults can produce similar symptoms. Repair guidance should distinguish the failed component from a maintenance issue or a system that is nearing replacement. The equipment fuel and model help define the checks required.

Diagnosis compares the thermostat call with the furnace sequence, including ignition, flame sensing, safety-switch response, and blower operation. The fuel type, model information, fault indicators, filter condition, and timing of the shutdown help narrow the cause. These observations matter because a no-start, an ignition failure, and a blower fault require different checks and should not be treated as the same repair.

  • Ignition, flame-sensor, pressure-switch, and blower-motor diagnosis.
  • Inspection of airflow, filters, burners, thermostats, and key components.
  • Repair-versus-replacement decisions based on fault and furnace condition.

Assessment and Scope

Questions for the Proposed Work

01

Separate ignition faults from airflow faults

A furnace that does not ignite may have a problem involving its igniter, flame sensor, or pressure switch, while a furnace that lights but moves little air points toward a different set of checks, such as the blower motor. Record any displayed fault code and describe what happens before the unit stops. Because these components involve combustion, safety controls, or internal electrical work, diagnosis and repair belong with a qualified service provider.

02

Use inspection findings to guide maintenance

Maintenance findings can include a restricted filter, dirty burners, airflow concerns, thermostat operation, or wear at key components. The appropriate work depends on the furnace model, manufacturer instructions, and what inspection reveals; a cleaning or adjustment is not a substitute for repairing a confirmed fault. Keep the model information and recent service records available, since they help distinguish recurring component problems from conditions that may be corrected through specified maintenance.

03

Compare repair with replacement on condition

A repair decision depends on the diagnosed fault, the furnace's age and condition, parts availability, and whether similar failures have recurred. Replacement becomes a separate consideration when the existing unit has extensive deterioration or repeated costly faults, not simply because one component failed. If comparing options, ask what the repair addresses, what remains uncertain, and whether a replacement would require changes to venting, fuel connections, or the existing duct system.

Compare the next step

Ask for two separate answers: whether the existing furnace can operate properly and whether the completed addition can receive the heat it needs. A proposal should explain any supply and return changes, the calculated additional load, and how performance will be checked after the room is finished.
